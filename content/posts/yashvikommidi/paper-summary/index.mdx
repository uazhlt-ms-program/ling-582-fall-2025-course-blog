#### Description (LLM)

_In the LLM's words, what is this paper about?_

This research paper introduces a method called ALEX to analyze social media data for public health, addressing two key challenges: data imbalance and model limitations. 

First, the authors tackle the imbalance problem by using data augmentation and weighted loss training. They apply techniques like TextAttack to add more samples from the minority class, then fine-tune models like BERT with a weighted loss to prioritize these underrepresented classes. This ensures that the model learns to recognize rare events more effectively, which is crucial for tasks like monitoring rare health conditions or identifying outliers in large datasets.

Second, they leverage large language models (LLMs) like GPT-3.5 to correct predictions made by BERT. The LLM is prompted with the original text and the predicted label by BERT, then it explains whether the label is correct by looking for evidence in the text. If the LLM finds contradictory evidence, it identifies the error. This method enhances the accuracy and transparency of public health analysis, as LLMs can provide step-by-step reasoning and correct biases in BERT’s predictions. 

The ALEX framework outperforms other methods in public health-related text classification tasks at the SMM4H competition, demonstrating its effectiveness in making reliable and explainable predictions, which is vital for decision-making in areas like pandemic monitoring and policy development.

#### What are the authors proposing?

The authors propose a framework called ALEX to improve the analysis of social media data for public health applications, specifically in text classification tasks. Their solution directly addresses two critical challenges: data imbalance in social media datasets and the limitations of existing models like BERT and LLMs. Here is their proposal:

Balanced Data Training:

Data Augmentation: They apply techniques like TextAttack to artificially increase the number of minority class samples in the dataset. This is achieved by generating adversarial examples and oversampling to balance the dataset.
Weighted Loss Fine-Tuning: After augmentation, they fine-tune BERT models using a weighted loss function that assigns higher importance to the minority class during training. This ensures the model focuses on underrepresented data, improving its ability to learn from rare events.


Explainable and Corrective LLMs:

LLM Prompting: They use large language models (LLMs) like GPT-3.5 to correct predictions made by BERT. The LLM is prompted with the original text and the predicted label from BERT, along with specific instructions (e.g., labelling rules, examples) and representative examples for each task.
Label Correction Mechanism: The LLM evaluates the correctness of the predicted label by analyzing the text for evidence supporting or contradicting the label. If the LLM finds contradictions or insufficient evidence, it identifies the error and provides a step-by-step explanation of why the label is incorrect. This step reduces false positives and improves the accuracy of public health predictions.


Ablation Studies and Validation:

The authors conduct ablation studies to validate the effectiveness of their approach. By comparing different strategies, they demonstrate that combining balanced training with LLM correction leads to superior performance in public health tasks.
Experiments at the SMM4H 2023 competition, a public health-focused dataset challenge, confirm that ALEX outperforms other methods (e.g., BERT, XLNet) in tasks like COVID diagnosis and mental health analysis. The framework's ability to correct BERT predictions with LLMs ensures both high accuracy and transparency, which is critical for decision-making in public health contexts.



In summary, the authors propose a two-part solution to improve public health social media analysis: (1) balance the dataset through augmentation and weighted loss to address imbalances, and (2) use LLMs to correct BERT predictions with explainable reasoning. This dual approach enhances both the reliability and transparency of public health analysis, making it more effective for real-world applications.

### Experience using the LLM

_Describe your process for using the LLM.  How did the LLM perform?_

In this assignment, I used the SmolLM3-3B model on HuggingChat as my open-weight LLM. First, I converted the research paper from PDF to a text file using Python, because the hugging chat interface could only accept text file input. After that, I uploaded the text file version of the paper and, for each question in the assignment, I wrote a specific prompt and fed it to the model.

My process was very iterative. I started with fairly simple prompts and then looked at the answers the model gave. After each answer, I checked whether it was actually responding to the question and whether it matched the content of the paper. When the answers were incomplete, off-topic, or too generic, I tightened the prompts to get better behavior. For example, I added instructions like “use only information from the paper” and “don’t add anything that isn’t explicitly mentioned in the paper.” Over time, I tried to make the prompts as clear as possible so I could get the most accurate answers from the LLM.

Overall, I feel the LLM did well on high-level understanding. It consistently recognized that the paper proposes the ALEX framework for public health social media analysis, combining balanced training with LLM-based correction. For many of the improved prompts, the answers were mostly correct at a big-picture level. However, I noticed that for some of the more basic and direct questions and also the improved prompts, the answers were still not completely accurate. The model was also helpful for brainstorming how to phrase prompts and for generating structured answers.

As the answers were not always correct, I almost always had to go back to the original paper and double-check details. The model was strongest at giving high-level explanations in simple language, and weaker at precise technical details, such as exact metrics from the results tables, details of the evaluation setup, and the specific limitations and future work the authors mentioned.
 
#### Errors and limitations of the LLM 

_Where did it fall short or make mistakes?_


few of the limitations and errors that I encountered are :

Wrong or mixed-up numerical results: 

while working with this LLM, it often gave the numbers wrong when i asked for results. Sometimes it mixed values from different rows or tables , sometimes it pulled wrong values instead of correct ones. Even when I told it not to guess numbers, it still tried to give specific scores that didn’t match the paper.

False claim about missing baseline metrics: 

For one of the question, the model confidently said that the paper didn't provide a detailed baseline performance metrics. But I felt, it was wrong because the paper has a full results table with multiple baselines. It showed me that the LLM can make up statements about the paper itself and still sound very sure.

Misrepresenting the evaluation setup: 

The LLM kept describing the evaluation as being done on a “subset of the training data,” which is not how the paper explains it. The authors say they use the shared-task validation set, not some random subset of training. This mistake repeated several times, even after I refined the prompts.

Hallucinated limitations and future work: 

When I asked about limitations and future work, the model started adding things that were never explicitly mentioned, like exploring other transformer architectures, using the method in education, real-time monitoring, or regulatory compliance. Some of these ideas sounded plausible, but they were clearly not stated by the authors. So the LLM tended to fill in future work on its own instead of sticking to the paper.

Blurring motivation, approach, and takeaways: 

For questions such as “What is the motivation?” or “What are the takeaways according to the authors?”, the model sometimes mixed everything together. It would talk about methods or results instead of just focusing on motivation or conclusions. It also kept inserting themes like “scalability and efficiency” as if they were clearly highlighted by the authors, which they weren’t.

Ignoring strict prompt instructions: 

I tried to control the way few answers were given by writing very strict prompts like “only use information from this paper” and “don’t add anything that isn’t explicitly mentioned.” Even then, the LLM still generalized to new domains or added extra bullets that weren’t in the text. In a few answers, it even admitted something wasn’t explicitly mentioned while still including it.

Mixing “according to the authors” and “according to you”: 

When I asked for the takeaways “according to the authors” and “according to you,” the LLM didn’t always keep that distinction clear. It sometimes presented its own interpretations for example, emphasizing scalability as a main takeaway as if they were explicitly stated by the authors. This made it harder to trust that it was really reflecting the authors’ views.

Struggling with table-style content: 

Even though the text file I gave contained the content of the results tables, the LLM still seemed to struggle with the information. It often misread or misused the table information, which probably contributed to the wrong metrics and the confusion about baselines. This suggests that complex table formatting in plain text is still hard for the model.
