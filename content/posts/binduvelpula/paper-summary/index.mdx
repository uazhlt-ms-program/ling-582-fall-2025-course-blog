## Description

Stylomech extracts stylometric features from text pairs to verify authorship in English and Romanized Sinhala, enabling accurate author comparison with limited data.

## Motivation

This paper demonstrates a hybrid approach that can be extended with transformer embeddings for robust authorship verification in low-resource or multilingual settings.

This paper introduces "Stylomech," a framework designed for Authorship Verification (AV)—the task of determining whether two texts were written by the same person. The study focuses specifically on two linguistic contexts:
- English
- Romanized Sinhala (Sinhala written using English characters, often used in social media).
The paper addresses the challenge of identifying authorship in short text samples (like tweets or SMS) where traditional deep learning models might struggle due to data sparsity. It utilizes Computational Stylometry—the measurement of style markers like sentence length, vocabulary richness, and punctuation usage—to build a robust verification system.


#### What are the authors proposing?

##### Prompt

```markdown
What are the authors proposing?
```


The authors propose a comprehensive pipeline for authorship verification that relies on explicit feature engineering rather than "black-box" embeddings alone. Their proposal includes:

1. A Stylometric Feature Set: They propose extracting 55 distinct features categorized into:
   - Lexical features: (e.g., Average word length, vocabulary richness).
   - Syntactic features: (e.g., Punctuation frequency, function words).
   - Structural features: (e.g., Paragraph length).
2. Distance Metric Evaluation: They propose using absolute differences and cosine similarity between feature vectors of text pairs to quantify "style distance."
3. Romanized Sinhala Dataset: They introduce a novel dataset approach for Romanized Sinhala, addressing a gap in low-resource language forensics.


#### What is the motivation for the work?

```markdown

What is the motivation for the work?
```

The primary motivation is the rise of cybercrimes, such as phishing, harassment, and the spread of fake news, where the identity of the perpetrator is often hidden behind anonymous text.
Specific motivating factors include:
     - Data Scarcity: Deep learning models (like BERT) require massive datasets. In forensic scenarios, investigators often possess only short, limited writing samples.
     - Language Gaps: There is a significant lack of tools for "code-mixed" or transliterated languages like Romanized Sinhala, which is prevalent in Sri Lankan online communication.
     - Interpretability: In legal contexts, explainable features (e.g., "the suspect uses commas frequently") are often preferred over opaque neural network probabilities.

#### What is the approach or innovation?

##### Prompt

```markdown
prompt here
What is the approach or innovation?
```
The core innovation is the Stylomech Framework, which functions as a comparator.

The Approach:
1. Preprocessing: Cleaning text (removing URLs, emojis) while preserving stylometric markers like capitalization and punctuation.
2. Feature Extraction: The system extracts a vector of numerical values representing the writing style for Text A and Text B.
3. Vector Combination: Instead of feeding raw text to a classifier, they feed the difference between the two feature vectors (Feature Vector A - Feature Vector B).
4. Classification: They employ machine learning classifiers (Random Forest, XGBoost, SVM) to predict "Same Author" or "Different Author" based on these differences.
Innovation:
The application of these techniques specifically to Romanized Sinhala, creating a precedent for analyzing transliterated South Asian languages in forensic contexts

The authors achieved high performance metrics, demonstrating that stylometry remains competitive even against complex deep learning models for this specific task.
Key Results:
- English Dataset: The model achieved an accuracy of approximately 88.2% using the Random Forest classifier.
- Romanized Sinhala: The model achieved an accuracy of 94.7%, significantly outperforming baselines.
Comparison:
Compared to traditional Bag-of-Words (BoW) or simple TF-IDF approaches, Stylomech showed superior performance in handling short texts. While they did not extensively benchmark against Large Language Models (LLMs) in this specific paper, their results suggest that for limited data, stylometric engineering is more computationally efficient and comparably accurate to Transformer-based methods.

Without this, it is hard to say if it beats the current state-of-the-art (SOTA).

The authors conclude that:
1. Stylometry is Robust: Explicit linguistic features are highly effective for authorship verification, even in short texts.
2. Language Independence: The same set of stylometric features works surprisingly well for both English and Romanized Sinhala, suggesting that "style" is a universal cognitive fingerprint.
3. Classifier Importance: Tree-based models (Random Forest, XGBoost) generally outperformed linear models (SVM) and distance-based models (KNN) for this tabular feature data.

Remaining Problems:

Topic Bias: Stylometric models risk learning topics (e.g., Sports vs. Cooking) instead of invariant style markers.
Scalability: The quadratic growth of pairs N^2 poses computational challenges for large-scale verification.
Baseline Gap: Lack of strong transformer comparisons (like mBERT) for Romanized Sinhala leaves the neural vs. stylometric question open.
