## Description
This paper is a survey of existing research on using Large Language Models for studying Lexical Semantic Change (LSC). Lexical Semantic Change is the shift in meaning of a given word over time, and the paper outlines various approaches that have been developed to use LLMs to measure this change. Additionally, the paper groups existing approaches for using LLMs on LSC problems based on what type of linguistic change is being detected, as well as by form-based, sense-based, and ensemble-based approaches.

After a general introduction to the topic, a definition of different types of language change detection, and formalization of notation to be used to demonstrate the various methods of assessment of lexical change, the paper proposes its centrally structuring "classification framework.” In brief, the authors propose to divide existing approaches for using LLMs to study LSC into form-based approaches—approaches that focus on a single, often dominant, sense of a target word—and sense-based approaches—those approaches that utilize representations of all uses of the target word, and that, thus, can measure shifts in distinct meanings of the word as well as lost/gained meanings.

With this distinction in place, the paper proceeds to survey the existing LSC literature, first the form-based approaches, then the sense-based approaches, and finally ensemble approaches that combine some properties of each. In each section, the authors compare the scholarship that has employed the given approach along a number of features of the research and algorithm employed (learning modality, LLM used, change function, corpus language, etc.). Novel approaches from particular papers are briefly summarized. 

The discussion section of this survey paper compares the performance of 29 different papers’ approaches on various benchmarks and draws some broad conclusions. The authors note that form-based approaches are more popular and perform better on almost every benchmark, but caution that sense-based approaches are "disadvantaged by [the] structure” of the benchmarks, as they require a single change value to be compared against a gold score. This, obviously, is not what sense-based approaches are best suited for.

The authors conclude by examining the persistent challenges of scalability, interpretability, and robustness, with a particularly notable point (to me, at least) being that all of the examined approaches could produce measures of change for a given target word, but that none of them could examine the change of an entire vocabulary and produce (for example) a list of 10 most and least changed words due to the computational expense. 

One of the final paragraphs held particular importance to me and my research interests, which I will quote in full:

"So far, LSC through contextualized embeddings is still a theoretical problem not yet integrated in real application scenarios such as historical information retrieval, lexicography, linguistic research, or social-analysis. Among the existing use cases, semantic change has been examined by Reference [16] to investigate sudden events that radically alter public opinion on a topic, and by References [95, 102] to explore shifts in olfactory perception and changes in the descriptions of smells over time. We expect that further use cases and experiences will be developed and shared in the next [sic] future." 

After exploring references 16, 95, and 102, I found that all of these papers utilized static embeddings! This may mean that, at least to the knowledge of the authors of this survey paper, no one is yet using these techniques for “historical information retrieval, lexicography, linguistic research, or social-analysis.” This is exactly my hope for my research, and it’s interesting to see, what seems to me at least, as a glaring lack in an application of these tools for types of research that they may be particularly well-suited for!
