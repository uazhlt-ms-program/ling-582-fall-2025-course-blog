#### What LLM did you select (model and version) and why?

<br />

For the project I selected the HuggingFace SmolLM3-3B for the paper task over similar sized models such as Qwen2.5-3B, or larger models such as Llama 3.2 or Google's Gemma. The biggest reason for selecting the model was the balance it has between performance and efficiency along with its easy integration into the HuggingChat platform to provide prompts. 

Benefits I was seeking with the model are detailed below:

- ***Ultra-Efficiency***: The model is specifically designed to be as high performing as possible while remaining efficient to even the most resource constrained environments. Authors of the model note that this is a growing ask among LLM users (Bakouch et al., 2025). 

- ***Strong Performance***: In a comparison between three Qwen LLMs, Llama 3.2, and Gemma3, the SmolLM3-3B Base model placed third in terms of benchmark performance despite being the second fastest among the six models tested, highlighting the balance between performance and efficiency (Bakouch et al., 2025). 

- ***Long Context Window***: The inference size of 128,000 tokens was one of the largest benefits as it allows for more nuanced and coherent answers to be developed. This is necessary given that we are working with academic papers that have dense amounts of inter-related information across long spans of text. 

- ***Reasoning Ability***: SmolLM3-3B has the ability to incorporate a reasoning feature for responses. This was of particular interest as the questions prepared for the paper are longer and involve a multi-step reasoning processes to achieve the best results. 

Some of the initial concerns I had for the model are detailed below: 

- ***Lower Parameter Size***: Given the relatively low parameter count compared to larger models from Meta and Google, there were concerns about the model’s ability to realistically handle a complex prompt and make meaning from the entire paper. The larger pre-trained models typically have stronger performance on benchmarks and handle nuanced prompts with more caution. 

- ***Hallucinations***: Since the paper includes over 10 pages of text with tables and diagrams, there was concern as to whether the model would be able to effectively fit the paper within its context window and produce high quality answers to various parts.

##### AI Response

> The authors propose a Transformer-based model, **PatchTST**, designed for long-term time series forecasting. Their key innovations include:
>
> 1. **Patch-based Input Segmentation**:  
>   - **Patching Mechanism**: They divide multivariate time series into non-overlapping patches of fixed length (e.g., 16 time steps with a stride of 8). This reduces the input size from \( L \) (original sequence length) to \( \approx L/S \), significantly lowering computational complexity.  
>   - **Local Semantic Information**: Patches retain local patterns, enabling the model to learn meaningful temporal relationships at different scales, which is critical for long-term forecasting.  
>
> 2. **Channel-Independence Design**:  
>   - **Independent Processing of Channels**: Each channel (e.g., a single time series feature) is processed independently, ensuring that information from one channel does not interfere with another. This allows the model to focus on specific features without cross-channel noise.  
>   - **Adaptability**: PatchTST adapts to diverse time series behaviors, as each series can learn distinct attention patterns, improving robustness against unrelated or conflicting data.  
>
> 3. **Longer Look-Back Windows**:  
>   - **Strategic Downsampling**: By downsampling the input sequence (e.g., every 4 steps) and padding with the last value, PatchTST can use a longer look-back window (\( L \)) without increasing computational costs. This enhances the model's ability to capture historical dependencies.  
>
> 4. **Self-Supervised Pre-training**:  
>   - **Masked Autoencoder**: They use masked autoencoders to learn abstract representations by randomly masking patches and training the model to reconstruct them. This avoids the need for large labeled datasets and produces high-quality features for downstream tasks.  
>
> 5. **Transfer Learning**:  
>   - **Cross-Dataset Transfer**: Pre-trained models on one dataset (e.g., Electricity) are fine-tuned on others (e.g., Weather, Traffic), reducing the need for extensive labeled data. This approach leverages learned representations for improved performance across tasks.  
>
> 6. **Efficiency and Robustness**:  
>   - **Reduced Complexity**: PatchTST's design minimizes memory and computational demands, making it scalable for large datasets.  
>   - **Robustness to Overfitting**: By using downsampling and padding, it avoids overfitting while preserving critical temporal information. Instance normalization further mitigates distribution shifts between training and testing data.  
>
> The authors demonstrate that PatchTST outperforms traditional Transformer models in multivariate time series forecasting, especially on large datasets. Their approach combines patching and channel independence to retain semantic information, reduce complexity, and enable effective learning from longer sequences, making it a robust and efficient foundation model for future work.

In the paper the authors propose a custom rendition of a transformer encoder model that is designed specifically for sequential time-series data. Instead of framing a time series as a sequence of individual events, parts of the overall series are tokenized similar to a language model for forecasting tasks. 

Conceptually the idea was noted to be an extension of deep learning based forecasting developments that have more recently been developed. These include the use of standard feed-forward networks, convolutional networks, and other transformer based methods. Despite these models realizing promising results for the application of deep learning in the field, they have been equally matched by more simple linear models (Zeng et al., 2022). 

The authors note that aspects of the transformer model such as the attention mechanism and the way data is represented in tokens presents many opportunities to make new breakthroughs in forecasting performance that cannot be achieved with linear models. They specifically propose two new approaches utilizing transformers for forecasting through methods called patching and channel independence. 

Patching involves creating local context embeddings from the larger sequences that mimics the tokenization phase, and channel independence involves processing multi-variate sequences independently versus a standard channel mixing process. Additionally, the use of language model-like pre-training processes such as token masking was proposed by the authors as a method for richer interpretations of general time series signals to boost the performance of downstream tasks.

> The authors' motivation stems from the limitations of Transformers in handling long-term time series forecasting, where their inherent design struggles with large sequence lengths and high computational costs. Key inspirations for their methods include:
>
> 1. **Patch-based Optimization**:  
>   - **Insight**: Patching, a technique used in computer vision and NLP to reduce memory and improve efficiency, was adapted to Transformers. By segmenting time series into non-overlapping patches, the authors reduce the input size from \( L \) to \( \approx L/S \), which is critical for Transformers' \( O(N^2) \) complexity.  
>   - **Adaptation**: This approach is inspired by the success of patching in other domains (e.g., ViT, BEiT) to handle large-scale data with limited resources.
>
> 2. **Channel Independence**:  
>   - **Insight**: Channel independence, where each channel (e.g., a single time series feature) is processed independently, was used in traditional methods like TCN and LogTrans. The authors extend this to Transformers, ensuring cross-channel noise is mitigated.  
>   - **Adaptation**: Channel independence leverages the efficiency of Transformers while avoiding cross-channel interference, a key advantage over channel-mixing approaches that blend all channels into a single input.
>
> 3. **Longer Look-Back Windows**:  
>   - **Insight**: Downsampling (e.g., sampling every 4 steps) and padding with the last value allow the model to use a longer historical context (\( L \)) without increasing computational demands. This is inspired by techniques in time series analysis (e.g., ARIMA) that use sparse data for efficiency.  
>   - **Adaptation**: Downsampling is a pragmatic solution to enable Transformers to learn from longer sequences while maintaining scalability.
>
>4. **Self-Supervised Learning for Pre-training**:  
>   - **Insight**: Masked autoencoders, a staple in NLP for learning embeddings from unlabeled data, were applied to Transformers. This addresses the scarcity of labeled time series data, a common challenge in generative and forecasting tasks.  
>   - **Adaptation**: The authors adapt this self-supervised strategy to Transformers, enabling effective pre-training on unlabeled data and transfer learning across datasets.
>
> 5. **Efficiency and Robustness**:  
>   - **Insight**: Traditional Transformer models often overfit due to their large receptive fields. The authors use downsampling and padding to reduce overfitting while preserving temporal context, inspired by sparse convolutional networks (e.g., TCN) that mitigate overfitting through sparsity.  
>   - **Adaptation**: This approach balances computational efficiency with the need for robustness, addressing the trade-off between model complexity and data utilization.
>
> **Why These Approaches?**  
> The authors' work is driven by the need to **scale Transformers for time series forecasting** while **avoiding overfitting** and **maintaining computational feasibility**. Traditional Transformer models struggle with long sequences due to their inherent design, which is compounded by the lack of labeled data in many time series tasks. By combining patching, channel independence, downsampling, and self-supervised learning, they create a more efficient and adaptable framework that outperforms existing methods. This approach bridges the gap between the Transformers' strengths in sequence modeling and their limitations in temporal data, making it a viable solution for long-term forecasting and foundation models in time series analysis.

>
> 1. **Supervised Forecasting**:  
>   - PatchTST/64 (with a longer look-back window and more patches) achieves **21.0% reduction in MSE** and **16.7% in MAE** compared to the best Transformer baselines on large datasets (Weather, Traffic, Electricity).  
>   - On smaller datasets (ILI), PatchTST/42 (default model) still outperforms other Transformers by **20.2% in MSE** and **16.4% in MAE**.  
>
> 2. **Self-Supervised Pre-training**:  
>   - PatchTST outperforms state-of-the-art self-supervised methods like BTSF, TS2Vec, TNC, and TS-TCC by **34.5%-48.8%** in forecasting accuracy, demonstrating its effectiveness in learning abstract representations.  
>
> 3. **Transfer Learning**:  
>   - Pre-training PatchTST on Electricity and fine-tuning on other datasets yields **MSE improvements of 48.5%-48.8%**, slightly exceeding supervised training in some cases.  
>
> 4. **Ablation Study**:  
>   - **Channel independence** is critical: PatchTST with both patching and channel independence (P+CI) outperforms all other variants, including standalone patching (P) and channel-mixing (CI).

>

> - **PatchTST** is the most effective Transformer model for multivariate time series forecasting, especially when combined with **channel independence**.  
> - **Channel independence** reduces cross-channel interference and allows Transformers to focus on local patterns, enhancing robustness.  
> - **Patch length** and **look-back window** are trade-offs: Longer \( L \) improves performance but requires downsampling to maintain efficiency.

> The authors identified several key **remaining challenges** for applying transformers to non-text scenarios and time series forecasting, which they noted as future areas of work:
>
> 1. **Long-term dependency resolution**: Transformers struggle with capturing long-term dependencies in time series due to their reliance on sequential information. While the authors used downsampling and padding to manage this, **efficient attention mechanisms** tailored to temporal coherence could enhance performance.
>
> 2. **Cross-channel interactions**: Channel independence in PatchTST avoids cross-channel interference but **integrating cross-channel correlations** (e.g., time series with similar patterns across channels) remains unexplored.
>
> 3. **Temporal coherence of patches**: The model assumes sequential patches (e.g., overlapping or consecutive patches) but **ensuring temporal coherence** (e.g., consistent temporal relationships between patches) could improve accuracy.
>
> 4. **Self-supervised learning for time series**: While self-supervised pre-training is effective, **more sophisticated methods** (e.g., contrastive learning, attention mechanisms) might better capture abstract representations in time series.
>
> 5. **Non-linear relationship modeling**: Transformers might not fully capture **non-linear temporal dependencies** (e.g., seasonality, trends) that are common in time series, suggesting the need for **non-linear attention mechanisms**.
>
> 6. **Temporal consistency in transfer learning**: Ensuring **temporal alignment** between pre-trained and fine-tuned models on different datasets is a challenge that the authors didn't address.
>
> 7. **Efficient attention for time series**: Transformers use a vanilla attention mechanism, but **attention mechanisms optimized for time series** (e.g., dynamic attention, hierarchical attention) could improve scalability and accuracy.
>
> These issues highlight the need for **transformer-specific attention and attentional architectures** for time series, as well as **holistic approaches** to temporal coherence and cross-channel interactions, which the authors see as critical for future advancements in the field.

Many of these challenges are surrounding the practicality of utilizing a transformer model for such tasks when much simpler renditions can often lead to comparable results. 

A major area of challenge for these models that was not covered in depth by the authors is the use of these models in scenarios where the data does not inherently have a stable cyclical nature or a low amount of random noise. In the paper all models were tested on weather, utilities use, and traffic patterns which all have heavy cyclical and predictable natures and less noise than something like a time series of human behaviors. It is relatively unclear how the model would perform in scenarios where random fluctuations in the data occur on a more frequent basis.

As mentioned, transformer models are also much more complex and computationally intensive than standard forecasting models making them prone to a host of issues. These include the possibility of overfitting to small and homogenous datasets, the additional skill level required to ensure they are being properly utilized, and the cost that is required for training and inference tasks. These aspects are more practical than scientific in nature but still affect the potential success of the approach in more novel scenarios. 

Finally, the authors' adaptation of language model like pre-training processes displayed signs of performance improvement but not to the same extent as experienced for text based language tasks. These processes are critical for the broader success of a language based transformer model but may not produce the same leap in terms of general forecasting performance, with basic supervised learning approaches still producing impressive results. Focusing on the improvement of these concepts has substantial potential for increasing the overall performance of transformer based time series models in a broad spectrum of domains with much less available data.
