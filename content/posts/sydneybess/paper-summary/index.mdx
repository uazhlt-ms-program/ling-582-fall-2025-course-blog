## Description

This paper is about the use of Optical Character Recognition(OCR) on indigenous languages of the Americas. OCR is the process of extracting text (essentially digitizing it) from an image, or in this case, scanned typed PDFs. Most language resources for Indigenous low-resource languages come from handwritten fieldnotes or older books that taught the language, which have no digital copies. But it takes a long time to go through and type in everything from the books; many people don't have the time.


This is why the article discusses using OCR and training the model on limited annotated or even synthetic data. They selected PDFS from four indigenous languages of South America: Asháninka, Shipibo-Konibo, Yanesha, and Yine. Their data consisted of 454 scanned pages from 89 books, totaling 3,900 tokens, along with tables and graphs, both typed and handwritten. Next, they annotated the data (adding the typed words). Next, they preprocessed the PDFs using software to remove noise such as scanned marks, stains, and page numbers.


Then comes the OCR process; the article specifically discusses two popular OCR models, Google Vision and Tesseract.  They ran these models in Latin script and used Character Error Rate (CER) and Word Error Rate (WER) as metrics to evaluate model performance.


The different models had strengths and weaknesses: Google Vision didn't do a good job of maintaining the correct order and format of multicolumn text, while Tesseract did. On the other hand, Tesseract had more character insertions (e.g., mistaking one unique character for two common characters) than Google Vision. Other issues included word boundary issues (adding spaces where there shouldn't be, or treating single spaces as new paragraphs), which predominantly affected Google Vision.


Now that the basic OCR was complete, they returned to manually correcting data and fed it into sequence-to-sequence models to reduce errors. These included Denorm, Ensemble, and Single Source.


The results were overall better, with significantly improved accuracy on diacritics, though a few characters were deleted or inserted.

Custom Post-Processing:
Linguistic validation: Rules (e.g., regex patterns for Indigenous verb tenses, tonal markers) flag high-confidence errors.

Post-processing ensures usability: Without community involvement, OCR errors would misrepresent linguistic nuances (e.g., a misread verb tense could alter cultural knowledge).
This approach turns OCR from a technical task into a collaborative preservation tool—directly addressing the authors’ motivation for community-centered digitization.
